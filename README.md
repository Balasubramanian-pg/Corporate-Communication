# Corporate-Communication

What you're trying to develop is more than the ability to give status updates. You're trying to build a **communication reflex: the ability to convert completed work into a concise, meaningful update without having to consciously construct every sentence.**

Imagine you're in a meeting. Someone asks:

"Where are we with the data validation?"

Instead of explaining everything you did, you instinctively know:

- What information matters?
- What has changed since the last update?
- What was the outcome?
- Why should the room care?
- What happens next?

Eventually, this should become automatic.

I would approach this through six mental models. Each trains a different part of that reflex.

# **The six mental models**

## **1. BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front**

Train your opening sentence

THE PRINCIPLE

Lead with the conclusion, not the story.

People in meetings shouldn't have to listen to 45 seconds of explanation to discover whether something is completed.

**Weak communication:**

"I was working on the validation yesterday. I checked the different tables, compared the records, and looked at some discrepancies. I also made a few changes..."

**Strong communication:**

"The data validation is complete. We identified and resolved three discrepancies, and the dataset is ready for downstream processing."

The difference is the order of information.

Your mental trigger:

**If someone remembers only my first sentence, will they understand the situation?**

Practice: Start every work update with one of these:

- "The task is complete..."
- "We've identified the issue..."
- "The pipeline is now operational..."
- "The report is ready for review\..."
- "We're currently blocked by..."

## **2. Delta Thinking — Communicate What Changed**

Train your information selection

THE PRINCIPLE

An update is not a description of everything you did. It is a description of what changed.

Think of your previous update as the baseline.

Your current update should communicate the difference between then and now.

**Example: Data pipeline**

BEFORE

**Pipeline failing**

Duplicate records and inconsistent ingestion.

AFTER

**Pipeline restored**

Duplicates addressed and ingestion validated.

**The update**

"We've resolved the duplicate-record issue and restored the ingestion pipeline. Initial validation is successful."

Your mental trigger:

**What is different now compared with the last time I spoke about this?**

This prevents you from repeating information that everyone already knows.

## **3. Action → Result → Impact**

Train your ability to communicate value

THE PRINCIPLE

Completing an action isn't the same as communicating its value.

You need to connect three things:

**ACTION**

What did I do?

**RESULT**

What happened because of it?

**IMPACT**

Why does that matter?

**Example:**

- Action: Optimised the Databricks transformation.
- Result: Reduced execution time from 40 minutes to 18 minutes.
- Impact: The downstream reporting process can now start earlier.

**Meeting update:**

"We've optimised the Databricks transformation, reducing execution time from 40 to 18 minutes. This allows downstream reporting to begin earlier."

Your mental trigger:

**What did my work actually improve?**

One important distinction: Never invent an impact. If you haven't measured it, communicate the result and describe the potential impact as a possibility.

## **4. The Three-Horizon Model — Past, Present, Future**

Train your ability to establish direction

THE PRINCIPLE

A good update should orient the listener in time.

- Past: What have we completed?
- Present: Where do things stand?
- Future: What happens next?

This is particularly useful when multiple people are collaborating on a project.

**Example:**

"The historical claims data has been ingested and validated. We're currently reconciling the financial fields against the source. Once that's complete, we'll move into the benefit calculation stage."

Notice what this accomplishes:

1. Establishes progress.
2. Explains the current position.
3. Gives people an expectation of what comes next.

Your mental trigger:

**Where were we, where are we, and where are we going?**

You don't always need all three horizons. For a straightforward completed task, a single sentence might be sufficient.

## **5. The Information Compression Model — One Breath, One Message**

Train conciseness

THE PRINCIPLE

Your job isn't to transfer all the information you possess.

Your job is to transfer the minimum information necessary for someone to understand the situation and make a decision.

Imagine you have 60 seconds to explain something.

Can you compress it into 20 seconds without losing the essential meaning?

Consider this:

**Uncompressed:**

"I was looking into the issue with the Medicaid rebate calculations. There were some inconsistencies in the source data, and I spent some time checking the calculation logic against the reference documentation. I found that the problem was related to the treatment of the unit rebate amount, so I corrected that and reran the calculation."

**Compressed:**

"We identified an inconsistency in the Medicaid rebate calculation, traced it to the unit rebate amount logic, and corrected it. The recalculated output now matches our expected methodology."

Same essential information. Much less cognitive effort for the listener.

Your mental trigger:

**What can I remove without changing the meaning?**

A useful constraint:

- Routine update: 10–15 seconds.
- Important update: 20–30 seconds.
- Complex issue: 30–60 seconds, followed by questions.

These are practice targets, not rigid rules.

## **6. Closed-Loop Communication — Never Leave the Listener Guessing**

Train accountability

THE PRINCIPLE

A task being completed doesn't necessarily mean the communication loop is closed.

The listener needs to know whether:

- The task is complete.
- Something remains unresolved.
- Someone else needs to take action.
- A decision or confirmation is required.

**Example:**

"The validation is complete, and the output has been shared with the analytics team. We're waiting for their confirmation before moving the model into production."

This is significantly better than:

"The validation is complete."

Why? Because the second statement leaves the room wondering what happens next.

Your mental trigger:

**Who needs to do what after I finish speaking?**

This model is especially important when working across engineering, analytics, commercial and business teams.

# **Bringing all six models together**

Let's imagine you're working on a pharmaceutical commercial analytics project.

You have just completed the Medicaid rebate calculation validation.

Here's how each mental model influences your update:

| Mental model            | What you think about      |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------- |
| 1. BLUF                 | What's the conclusion?    |
| 2. Delta                | What changed?             |
| 3. Action–Result–Impact | What value did we create? |
| 4. Three Horizons       | What's next?              |
| 5. Compression          | What can I remove?        |
| 6. Closed Loop          | Who needs to act now?     |

## **The final meeting update**

"The Medicaid rebate calculation validation is complete. We identified and corrected an inconsistency in the unit rebate amount logic, and the recalculated results now align with our reference methodology. I've shared the outputs with the commercial analytics team, and we're awaiting their sign-off before proceeding to the next stage."

Communication objective

Closed loop

Conclusion → Change → Result → Next action

# **How to make this second nature**

Here's where I would challenge you a little.

Reading these six models will not make you a concise communicator. You need to train the behaviour repeatedly, especially when there is no pressure to communicate.

I recommend a 14-day exercise.

## **14-day communication training**

A small daily exercise designed to build automaticity.

1. **Days 1–2: BLUF**

   After completing a task, describe the outcome in one sentence.
2. **Days 3–4: Delta Thinking**

   Explain only what changed since your previous update.
3. **Days 5–6: Action–Result–Impact**

   Connect your work to its measurable outcome.
4. **Days 7–8: Three Horizons**

   Practise explaining completed work, current status and next steps.
5. **Days 9–10: Compression**

   Take a 60-second explanation and reduce it to 20 seconds.
6. **Days 11–12: Closed Loop**

   Always identify the next owner, action or decision.
7. **Days 13–14: Integration**

   Give spontaneous 20-second updates using all six models.

After every meaningful work activity, ask yourself these questions:

## **One final thought**

I want you to internalise this distinction:

**An inexperienced communicator describes activity. An effective communicator communicates progress. A strong communicator communicates progress, implications and what happens next.**

Your ultimate goal isn't to memorise six templates and mechanically repeat them.

It's to develop an instinct where, immediately after completing an action, your brain naturally processes:

1. What happened?
2. What changed?
3. Why does it matter?
4. What's next?

The six mental models are training wheels. Eventually, you won't need to consciously recall them.

You'll simply know what to say, how much to say, and when to stop.
